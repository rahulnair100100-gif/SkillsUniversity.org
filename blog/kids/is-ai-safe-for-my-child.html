AI Safety

Is AI Safe for My Child?

Neither scaremongering nor a brochure — the real risks of AI for children, and a checklist you can use today.

SkillsUniversity Team • July 21, 2026 • 7 min read

Every parent researching AI courses for their child eventually hits the same wall of worry: is this actually safe? Fake images, made-up "facts," and data a child should never share are all real risks. Here is an honest assessment — neither scaremongering nor a sales brochure — plus a practical checklist you can use today.

The short answer

AI can be safe for children aged 10 to 16 with the right tools and adult guidance. The biggest risks are not robots — they are misinformation, fake images, oversharing personal data, and using AI as a thinking-replacement. All four can be managed by teaching your child to spot them, which is a skill, not a setting.

The real risks (and the ones that are overblown)

Let's separate the genuine concerns from the noise. The risks worth your attention with a 10 to 16 year old are:

  • Misinformation. AI can state wrong things with total confidence. A child who trusts every answer is the one at risk.
  • Fake images and deepfakes. Convincing fakes are now easy to make and easy to be fooled by.
  • Personal data. Children may type names, schools, addresses or photos into a chat without realising where it goes.
  • Over-reliance. Using AI to avoid thinking — the homework-shortcut trap — quietly erodes real learning.

What is less of a concern than headlines suggest: age-appropriate, mainstream AI tools used with a parent's oversight are, broadly, about as safe as any other connected device — safe enough to be worthwhile, not risk-free.

The line that matters most

A child using a general chatbot completely unsupervised is not appropriate under 13. A child using age-appropriate tools with a parent who sets up the account and checks in is in a genuinely different, and far safer, situation. Supervision is the difference.

Why teaching beats blocking

The instinct is to block — VPN filters, banned apps, locked devices. It rarely works for long, and it teaches nothing. Children find their way around restrictions, and a child who has only ever been blocked has no judgement of their own when they finally get access.

The durable form of safety is internal: a child who has been taught what never to share, how to check a claim before believing it, and how to spot a fake needs fewer parental controls over time, because they have internalised good judgement. That is the entire philosophy behind a safety-first AI course — safety as something taught, not just mentioned.

A practical AI safety checklist for parents

  1. You own the account. Any sign-ups are done by an adult, on your email.
  2. Preview it yourself. Watch or try anything before your child does.
  3. Set the data rule early. Full name, address, school, passwords and photos never go into a chat.
  4. Teach the fact-check reflex. "Where did that come from? How could we check it?"
  5. Check in weekly. Ask what they made and what they asked, without turning it into surveillance.
  6. Choose no-community tools. No open chat or forum means nothing for a stranger to reach your child through.

What to look for in a safe AI course for kids

If you are evaluating any AI program for your 10 to 16 year old, safe options tend to share these traits: the account is parent-purchased and parent-supervised; there is a full, dedicated module on safety rather than a throwaway line; honesty is taught explicitly; there is no live chat or community for strangers to enter; and you have full access to preview everything. AI Fundamentals for Kids was built around exactly these principles.

The bottom line

Is AI safe for your child? With age-appropriate tools, your supervision, and — crucially — a child taught to spot the real risks themselves, yes. Start supervised, build trust, and gradually extend independence. That is not just good AI parenting; it is good parenting.

This article is general guidance for parents, not a substitute for your own judgement about your individual child and the specific tools they use.
